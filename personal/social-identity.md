# Social Identity

Who I am *to other people*, and how deliberately that gets constructed. This
file has two halves: the theory of how social identity works, and the part
where I actually define mine.

Distinct from [Self-Map](self-map.md) — that one is who I am when nobody's
watching. This one is the interface.

---

## The theory

### Identity is claimed and granted

You can claim an identity, but it only becomes real when others grant it.
The gap between the two is where most social friction lives — claiming
more than is granted reads as pretension; being granted more than is
claimed is uncomfortable in a different way. Most of what looks like
"social skill" is managing the gap: making the claim legible enough, and
backing it with enough evidence, that granting it is easy.

### Social identity theory

People categorize themselves into groups, derive self-esteem from group
membership, and systematically favor the in-group. This happens on
arbitrary groupings, not just meaningful ones, which suggests the
mechanism is cheap and automatic rather than reasoned. Consequences: group
membership is load-bearing for self-worth in ways people rarely admit, and
threats to the group get processed as threats to the self.

### Roles and the expectations attached to them

A role is a bundle of expectations that attaches to a position, not to a
person — occupying it changes what people read into identical behavior.
Role conflict (occupying two roles with incompatible expectations) and
role strain (one role demanding more than can be given) are the standard
failure modes. See [Role, Duty & the Binding Figure](../trees/personal-philosophy/articles/role-duty-and-the-binding-figure.md).

### Impression management

Presentation is not optional — there's no un-presented version of yourself
available in a social context, only presentations you've thought about and
ones you haven't. The honest framing is not "authentic vs. performed" but
"which of my real facets gets foregrounded here, and did I choose it."

### Consistency, drift, and code-switching

Identity varies across contexts, and the question is whether that's
integrity failure or normal register-shifting. The usable test is probably
whether the different versions would embarrass each other if they met — a
change of register is fine; a change of commitments is not.

### Legibility

An identity that can't be summarized by someone else doesn't propagate.
People need a compressed version of you to carry into rooms you're not in.
What that compression says is largely determined by what you repeatedly
do in public, not by what you'd say about yourself.

## Open questions

- Is the claimed/granted gap something to close by claiming less or by
  demonstrating more — and does my answer change by context?
- How much of my self-worth actually rides on group membership, and would
  I notice if it were most of it?
- Is code-switching a failure of integrity or just competence? Where
  exactly is my line?
- Can a social identity be deliberately engineered without becoming
  hollow, and what's the tell that it's gone hollow?
- What do I do when a role I occupy demands something my own ethics
  refuses?
- Which parts of my identity are load-bearing for other people, such that
  changing them would cost someone else something?
