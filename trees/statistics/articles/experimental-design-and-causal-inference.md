# Experimental Design & Causal Inference

[← Statistics](../index.md) · `STAT_EXPDESIGN`

The part of statistics that decides whether a number means anything.
Randomization is the only mechanism that breaks confounding without
requiring you to know what the confounders are, which is why the RCT holds
its position — and also why the interesting arguments are all about what
to do when randomizing is impossible, unethical, or answers the wrong
question. Observational causal inference (matching, instrumental variables,
difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity, DAG-based
identification) works by making assumptions explicit enough to argue about,
not by removing them. The hard, permanent tension is internal versus
external validity: the cleaner the identification, the narrower and often
less interesting the population and setting it holds for.

## Open questions

- What assumptions is a given causal estimate really resting on, and which
  of them are testable even in principle?
- When is a well-argued observational design more trustworthy than a
  small, artificial, or badly-run randomized trial?
- How much should a clean local effect estimate move my beliefs about the
  policy or decision I actually face somewhere else?
- Is drawing the DAG a genuine discipline that surfaces hidden assumptions,
  or a way of dressing up assumptions you already wanted to make?
- What is the honest answer when someone asks "does X cause Y" and all I
  have is a well-identified effect of a specific intervention in a specific
  place?
- Where does the language of causation break down — feedback loops,
  equilibrium systems, things with no coherent counterfactual?
