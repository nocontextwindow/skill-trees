# Probability Theory

[← Statistics](../index.md) · `STAT_PROB`

The mathematics of uncertainty, and the layer where most later confusion is
actually seeded. The measure-theoretic machinery is settled and
uncontroversial; what is not settled is what a probability *means* — a
long-run frequency, a degree of belief, a physical propensity — and the
interpretation you pick silently determines which inference procedures even
make sense. The famous paradoxes (Monty Hall, Simpson's paradox, the
base-rate fallacy, Bertrand's paradox) are not curiosities; they are places
where an untrained intuition and the formalism disagree, and the formalism
wins. Independence and conditioning are where the real modelling
commitments hide, because assuming independence is a substantive empirical
claim that almost nothing verifies.

## Open questions

- Is probability a fact about the world or a fact about my information —
  and does anything I would actually do differ depending on the answer?
- How much measure theory is load-bearing for using statistics well, versus
  a rigour tax paid once and rarely cashed in?
- Where do independence assumptions quietly fail in the kinds of data I
  care about, and what is the cheapest way to detect that?
- Why do the classic paradoxes keep fooling people who know the math? What
  intuition is being violated, and can it be retrained?
- What does it even mean to assign a probability to a one-off event that
  will never be repeated?
- Do heavy-tailed distributions break the intuitions I built on Gaussian
  examples badly enough that I should default to distrusting means and
  variances?
