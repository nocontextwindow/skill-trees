# Institutional & Network Power

[← Power & Frame Control](../index.md) · `PWR_MODERN`

A great deal of modern power runs through neither formal hierarchy nor
ownership but through *position* — who is connected to whom, who sits
between otherwise disconnected groups, and which institutions confer
standing on whom. Network analysis makes part of this precise: brokerage
across structural holes, various measures of centrality, and the way
information and opportunity concentrate in a small number of connectors.
Institutions add a second layer by manufacturing credentials, defining what
counts as expertise, and setting defaults that nobody voted on and few
notice. Both forms are hard to see because neither appears on an org chart,
and both feel like merit from the inside — the person holding the position
experiences it as being asked, not as holding power over anyone.

## Open questions

- Is network position a form of power in itself, or only an advantage in
  acquiring power?
- Can positional power be built deliberately, or does designing for it
  destroy the trust it runs on?
- Who is accountable for an outcome produced by a network in which no one
  is in charge?
- How do institutions manufacture standing, and what happens to their power
  the moment people stop granting it?
- What does this kind of power look like from outside, and how would you
  detect it if you weren't already inside?
