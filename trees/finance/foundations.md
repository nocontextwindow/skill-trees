## Quantitative Foundations (`FIN_MATH`)

The math finance actually runs on: time value of money, present and future
value, compounding, discounting, and the basics of interest-rate
mechanics (simple vs. compound, nominal vs. real/inflation-adjusted, APR
vs. APY). The single idea worth over-learning here is that a dollar today
is worth more than a dollar in a year — not because of inflation alone, but
because of opportunity cost — since every discounted-cash-flow valuation,
bond price, and loan amortization schedule downstream is just this idea
applied repeatedly. Getting genuinely fluent with a financial calculator
or spreadsheet (NPV, IRR functions) at this stage saves a lot of confusion
later, since valuation and portfolio theory both just add more assumptions
on top of this same discounting machinery.

**Resources:**
- Richard Brealey, Stewart Myers & Franklin Allen, *Principles of Corporate Finance* (first few chapters)

## Accounting Fundamentals (`FIN_ACCT`)

The record-keeping language that finance is built on top of: the three
core financial statements (income statement, balance sheet, cash flow
statement), double-entry bookkeeping, and how they connect to each other
(net income flows into retained earnings on the balance sheet; the cash
flow statement reconciles accrual-basis income back to actual cash). The
distinction between accrual accounting and cash matters more than it
sounds like it should — a company can report strong "earnings" while
bleeding cash, which is exactly the gap that a lot of accounting fraud
(Enron being the canonical case) has historically hidden in. Fluency here
is the actual prerequisite for reading a 10-K critically rather than just
looking at a stock's price chart, which is what everything in the
Valuation & Risk section depends on.

**Resources:**
- Thomas Ittelson, *Financial Statements: A Step-by-Step Guide*
- Karen Berman & Joe Knight, *Financial Intelligence*
- Mary Buffett & David Clark, *Warren Buffett and the Interpretation of Financial Statements*

## Financial Markets & Instruments (`FIN_MARKETS`)

The landscape of what actually gets traded and where: equities, bonds,
derivatives (options, futures, swaps), and the market structure
(exchanges, market makers, order types, clearing) that makes trading them
possible. Understanding the basic instruments matters less than
understanding what each one is *for* — bonds price and transfer interest
rate and credit risk, options price and transfer volatility and asymmetric
payoff risk, and most of derivatives pricing (starting with Black-Scholes)
is really about pricing risk transfer rather than pricing "the thing
itself." This is also the node where monetary policy from the economics
tree becomes concretely visible: interest rate decisions ripple through
bond prices, equity valuations, and currency markets almost immediately.
