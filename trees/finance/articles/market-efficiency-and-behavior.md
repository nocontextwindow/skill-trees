# Market Efficiency & Behavior

[← Finance](../index.md) · `FIN_EFF`

Whether prices already incorporate available information, and what to
conclude when they visibly don't. The efficient market hypothesis is best
treated as a null hypothesis rather than a belief, and it comes with the
joint hypothesis problem: any test of efficiency is simultaneously a test
of the asset pricing model used to define what a correct price would be,
so the two can never be separated. Behavioral accounts explain why
mispricing arises, and the limits-to-arbitrage literature explains why it
persists — while implying that identifying a mispricing and being able to
profit from it are different achievements.

## Open questions

- If efficiency can never be tested apart from a pricing model, what is
  the concept still doing?
- Documented anomalies tend to shrink after publication — arbitraged
  away, or never there outside the sample?
- If markets are mostly efficient, what accounts for episodes where price
  and any plausible fundamental diverge for years?
- Limits to arbitrage explain why mispricing survives — so how does a
  practitioner ever act on finding one?
- What would I have to believe about myself to justify active
  management, and is that belief testable in advance?
- Is the informational efficiency of prices a public good that active
  managers are collectively paid to produce, and what happens as fewer
  of them do it?
