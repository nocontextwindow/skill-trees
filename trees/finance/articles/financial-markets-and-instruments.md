# Financial Markets & Instruments

[← Finance](../index.md) · `FIN_MARKETS`

What claims exist — equity, debt, currencies, commodities, funds,
contingent claims — and the machinery by which they change hands.
Instruments are mostly recombinations of a few primitives: a claim on
cash flows, a claim on assets, a promise contingent on something else.
The market structure around them, usually skipped in theory, does real
work: who provides liquidity, how orders meet, what a quoted price means
when a large fraction of volume comes from participants with no view on
the asset at all. Liquidity in particular is priced like a property of an
instrument but behaves like a property of conditions.

## Open questions

- What does a market price represent when much of the volume comes from
  participants with no view on the asset?
- Liquidity is treated as an attribute of an instrument but vanishes
  exactly when it's needed — how should that be modeled?
- Do digital assets constitute a genuinely new instrument class, or
  familiar claim structures — currency, equity, bearer instrument —
  rewrapped? Which of finance's existing tools carry over unchanged, and
  which quietly stop applying?
- What does market structure — exchanges, dealers, off-exchange venues,
  market makers — do to price formation that the theory ignores?
- Who bears the cost of intermediation, and how much of it is in the
  spread rather than the fee?
- Which instruments exist because they serve a real economic need, and
  which because of a tax, accounting, or regulatory boundary?
