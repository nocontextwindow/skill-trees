# Risk & Return

[← Finance](../index.md) · `FIN_RISK`

The claim that expected return is compensation for bearing risk, and the
long argument about what "risk" means. The tractable definition is
variance, which is why it won; the definition practitioners actually
worry about is permanent loss of capital, and the two come apart badly.
The CAPM is empirically weak and conceptually inescapable — almost every
subsequent framework is a patch on it — and the factor literature that
followed has an unresolved identification problem: a factor premium can
be compensation for risk, a persistent mispricing, or an artifact of
searching one finite history very thoroughly.

## Open questions

- Is risk variance, or the probability of permanent impairment? What
  changes downstream if I take the second definition seriously?
- CAPM fails empirically but frames everything — what should be kept from
  it and what discarded?
- Are factor premia compensation for risk, systematic mispricing, or
  data-mined artifacts? What evidence would separate these?
- How much of measured historical return is survivorship in the sample —
  of firms, of markets, of the period itself?
- Which risks am I compensated for bearing, and which am I simply exposed
  to for free?
- Is "risk tolerance" a stable preference or a description of how someone
  last behaved in a drawdown?
