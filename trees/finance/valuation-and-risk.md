## Valuation (`FIN_VALUE`)

Estimating what an asset (usually a company) is actually worth, as opposed
to what it's currently priced at. Discounted cash flow (DCF) analysis
values a business as the present value of the cash it will generate in the
future, and lives or dies on its assumptions (growth rate, discount rate,
terminal value) — a small change in the discount rate swings the answer
enormously, which is the classic critique of DCF's false precision.
Comparables ("comps") sidestep that by valuing a company relative to
similar public companies or recent transactions (P/E, EV/EBITDA multiples),
trading precision for market-grounded realism. The deepest question this
node sets up — are markets actually efficient, so that price already
reflects all available information (making active valuation work close to
pointless), or are there persistent, exploitable mispricings — is one
finance never fully resolves and this tree returns to repeatedly.

## Portfolio Theory & Risk (`FIN_PORT`)

How to combine assets into a portfolio that gets the best possible return
for a given level of risk. Modern Portfolio Theory (Markowitz) formalized
diversification: combining imperfectly-correlated assets reduces portfolio
variance without proportionally reducing expected return, which is the
rigorous version of "don't put all your eggs in one basket." The Capital
Asset Pricing Model then splits risk into diversifiable (idiosyncratic) risk,
which the market doesn't compensate you for since you can eliminate it for
free, and systematic (market) risk, which it does. The live debate worth
knowing: the efficient-market hypothesis says you can't beat a
risk-adjusted market return consistently through stock-picking, while a
body of empirical "anomalies" (value, momentum, size factors — the
Fama-French research) and behavioral finance both suggest real, if modest
and eroding, edges exist.

## Personal Finance & Wealth Building (`FIN_PERSONAL`)

Turning the theory into an actual personal plan: budgeting, emergency
funds, debt management, tax-advantaged accounts (401(k), IRA, HSA), asset
allocation appropriate to time horizon and risk tolerance, and the
mechanics of compounding over a working lifetime. The single highest-value
insight from decades of retail-investor research is boringly consistent:
low-cost, broadly diversified index funds held for decades reliably beat
the large majority of actively managed funds and the large majority of
individual stock-picking, once fees and behavioral mistakes (panic-selling
in downturns, chasing performance) are accounted for. The FIRE
(Financial Independence, Retire Early) movement pushes this further into a
lifestyle-design question — how much of current consumption is worth
trading for years of future freedom — which is really a personal-values
question wearing a spreadsheet.
