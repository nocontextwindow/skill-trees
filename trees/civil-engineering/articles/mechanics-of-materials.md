# Mechanics of Materials

[← Civil Engineering](../index.md) · `CE_MECH`

Stress, strain, and deformation in real members — bending, shear, torsion,
buckling, and combined loading. This is where the abstract forces of
statics become the internal state of a material, and where the classical
results (Euler-Bernoulli beam theory, Mohr's circle, Euler buckling) come
with assumptions that are stated once and then forgotten. Buckling is the
important lesson: a slender member can fail at a load far below its
material strength, so failure can be a geometry and stiffness problem
rather than a strength problem at all, and it is sensitive to imperfections
in a way strength failure is not. Stress concentrations at holes, notches,
and abrupt changes in section are where real components crack, and they are
invisible in a nominal stress calculation.

## Open questions

- Where do beam theory's assumptions break — and would I notice in a case
  where they had?
- If buckling makes failure a stiffness problem rather than a strength one,
  how much of design intuition built around strength is misplaced?
- How much does a stress concentration matter in practice, given that
  ductile materials redistribute and brittle ones don't?
- When is a finite-element result telling me about the structure and when
  about my mesh and boundary conditions?
- What is the honest relationship between the elastic analysis actually
  performed and the ultimate-strength behaviour actually relied on?
- Which of the classical closed-form results are still worth carrying in
  your head, and why those?
