# Transportation Engineering

[← Civil Engineering](../index.md) · `CE_TRANSPORT`

Geometric design, traffic flow, pavement, and network planning — the
discipline where the "material" being designed for is human behaviour, and
therefore adapts to the design. Induced demand is the canonical example:
added capacity changes travel choices until congestion returns, which
means the standard level-of-service framework can recommend interventions
that do not achieve their stated goal. Traffic flow has genuine physics
(shockwaves, capacity drop, the fundamental diagram) alongside genuine
behavioural indeterminacy. Design standards written for vehicle throughput
and those written for safety of all users often conflict directly — wider,
straighter, faster roads score well on one and badly on the other — and
much of current professional argument is about which objective a street
should serve.

## Open questions

- If demand responds to supply, what does capacity-based design actually
  achieve, and what should replace it as the objective?
- Whose safety and whose convenience do the standard design manuals
  optimize for, and is that visible in the documents themselves?
- How much of traffic behaviour is predictable physics and how much is
  irreducibly behavioural?
- What does induced demand imply for how any transport investment should be
  evaluated?
- How should a design account for a vehicle fleet and travel pattern that
  may change substantially within the asset's life?
- Where do modelled forecasts of future traffic have any track record of
  accuracy, and where are they known to be systematically wrong?
