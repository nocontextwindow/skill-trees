# Differential Equations

[← Mathematics](../index.md) · `MATH_DIFFEQ`

How to state a law of change, and what can be recovered from one. The
honest content of the subject is that almost nothing is solvable in closed
form — the standard course is a guided tour of the rare exceptions — and
that the questions that survive are qualitative: does a solution exist, is
it unique, is it stable, what does it do in the long run. Dynamical systems
theory grew out of accepting exactly that. This is also where determinism
and predictability come apart in plain view: a perfectly well-posed
deterministic system can be practically unpredictable.

## Open questions

- If closed-form solutions are the exception, what should this subject
  actually be teaching?
- What does a qualitative answer — a phase portrait, a stability claim —
  give me that a solution wouldn't?
- Determinism guarantees a unique trajectory but not predictability. How
  much does that distinction cost in practice?
- When is a numerical solution knowledge, and when is it a picture I've
  decided to trust?
- Why do so few equations arising from real physical systems have solutions
  anyone can write down? Is that about the systems or about our notation?
