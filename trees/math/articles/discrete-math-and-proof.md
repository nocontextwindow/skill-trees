# Discrete Math & Proof

[← Mathematics](../index.md) · `MATH_DISCRETE`

Where the shift happens from calculating to proving — the first point at
which mathematics is about justification rather than answers. Logic, sets,
counting, graphs, induction. The hard part isn't the content, which is
mostly elementary; it's the change of standard. Learning what counts as a
proof means absorbing a disciplinary norm as much as a formal one, since
essentially no published proof is formal in the logician's sense, and the
gap between "convincing to the right readers" and "mechanically checkable"
is where most of the interesting questions live.

## Open questions

- What actually makes a proof convincing, given that almost no real proof
  is formal?
- Is a proof an object or an act of communication? What follows for
  computer-generated proofs no human will read?
- Does a proof that verifies a statement without illuminating why it's true
  count as understanding it?
- Why is mathematical induction valid? Is there more to say than "the
  naturals are defined that way"?
- Which comes first for me — the sense that something is true, or the
  proof? What do I do when they conflict?
