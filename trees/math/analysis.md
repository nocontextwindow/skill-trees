## Real Analysis (`MATH_REALANAL`)

The rigorous re-derivation of everything calculus assumed: what a limit
actually is (epsilon-delta), what makes a function continuous or
differentiable, why the real numbers behave the way they do (completeness,
the least-upper-bound property), and which functions can and can't be
integrated. This is the class where a lot of otherwise-strong math students
hit a wall, because the skill required — constructing airtight proofs about
infinitesimal behavior — is qualitatively different from computational
fluency. The payoff is being able to answer "does this actually work, or
does it just work on the examples I've tried?" questions rigorously, and
it's the prerequisite for essentially all of higher pure mathematics
(topology, functional analysis, measure theory).

**Resources:**
- Walter Rudin, *Principles of Mathematical Analysis* ("Baby Rudin" — the classic, unforgiving standard)
- Stephen Abbott, *Understanding Analysis* (much gentler entry point)
- Terence Tao, *Analysis I* and *Analysis II*

## Topology (`MATH_TOPOLOGY`)

The study of properties that survive continuous deformation — stretching
and bending, but not tearing or gluing — which generalizes "closeness" and
"continuity" beyond the real numbers into abstract spaces. Point-set
topology builds the vocabulary (open sets, compactness, connectedness,
continuity in full generality); algebraic topology then attaches algebraic
invariants (the fundamental group, homology) to spaces to distinguish them
when geometry alone can't (this is the field that formalizes "a coffee cup
and a donut are the same shape, but a sphere isn't either of them"). It's
less immediately applicable than the rest of this tree, but it underlies
modern theoretical physics (gauge theory, string theory), data analysis
(topological data analysis, persistent homology), and a good chunk of
graduate-level pure math generally.
