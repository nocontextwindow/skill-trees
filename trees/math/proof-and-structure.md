## Discrete Math & Proof (`MATH_DISCRETE`)

The transition subject from "compute the answer" to "prove the statement
is true for all cases" — logic, set theory, combinatorics, graph theory,
and induction, plus the proof techniques (direct, contrapositive,
contradiction, induction) that everything in pure math and theoretical
computer science relies on. This is where most people encounter real
mathematical rigor for the first time, and it's genuinely a different
skill from computational calculus: precision of statement matters more
than facility with formulas. Graph theory and combinatorics also happen to
be the most directly useful discrete-math subfields for computer science
and algorithm design, so this tree pays off even for people who never touch
"pure" math again.

## Abstract Algebra (`MATH_ABSTRACT`)

The study of algebraic structures themselves — groups, rings, and fields —
stripped of any particular numerical content. A group is just a set with
an operation satisfying a handful of axioms (closure, associativity,
identity, inverses), and the payoff is that wildly different-looking
objects (integers under addition, symmetries of a square, permutations of
a deck of cards, the invertible matrices) turn out to obey exactly the same
structural laws. Galois theory — the historical motivation for group
theory — answers a genuinely surprising question: why there's no formula
(analogous to the quadratic formula) for solving general degree-5
polynomials. This is also the first place most students really internalize
"mathematics as the study of structure" rather than "mathematics as
calculation," which reframes a lot of what came before.

**Resources:**
