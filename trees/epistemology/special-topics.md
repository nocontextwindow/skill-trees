## Philosophy of Science: Induction / Falsifiability (`EP_SCI`)

Hume's problem of induction asks what justifies inferring future or unseen
cases from past observations, given that there's no non-circular argument
for the uniformity of nature. Popper's falsificationism tried to sidestep
the problem by making science about ruthless attempts at refutation rather
than inductive confirmation, but pure falsificationism struggles with the
Duhem-Quine thesis: any single hypothesis is tested only in conjunction
with auxiliary assumptions, so a failed prediction never uniquely
falsifies the hypothesis under test. Kuhn's paradigm shifts and
underdetermination of theory by evidence push further, questioning whether
theory choice is ever settled by evidence and logic alone.

**Resources:**
- Karl Popper, *The Logic of Scientific Discovery*
- Thomas Kuhn, *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*

## Social Epistemology: Testimony & Disagreement (`EP_SOCIAL`)

Most of what any individual knows rests on testimony rather than
first-hand perception or inference, which raises the reductionist question:
can testimonial justification be reduced to perception/memory/inference
about the speaker's reliability, or is trust in testimony a basic,
irreducible source (the anti-reductionist view, following Reid)? Miranda
Fricker's work on epistemic injustice shows how social identity (race,
gender) can unjustly deflate a speaker's credibility. The disagreement
literature asks what a rational agent should do on learning that an
epistemic peer holds the opposite view — split the difference
(conciliationism) or stick to one's guns if one's own reasoning seems sound
(steadfastness)?

**Status:** [ ]

**Resources:**
- Miranda Fricker, *Epistemic Injustice: Power and the Ethics of Knowing*
- Alvin Goldman, *Knowledge in a Social World*
- David Christensen, "Epistemology of Disagreement: The Good News"

## Formal / Bayesian Epistemology (`EP_BAYES`)

Replaces all-or-nothing belief with graded credences (subjective
probabilities) updated by Bayes' theorem as evidence comes in. Dutch book
arguments show that credences violating the probability axioms expose an
agent to a guaranteed loss across a set of bets, which is offered as a
pragmatic vindication of probabilism. Open problems include where the
priors come from in the first place, how to handle propositions you've
never considered (the problem of new theories), and whether Bayesian
updating is a genuinely descriptive account of rational belief change or
merely an idealized norm.

**Status:** [ ]

**Resources:**
- E.T. Jaynes, *Probability Theory: The Logic of Science*
- Richard Jeffrey, *Subjective Probability: The Real Thing*
- Michael Titelbaum, *Fundamentals of Bayesian Epistemology*

## Decision-Making Under Complexity (`EP_COMPLEX`)

Once justification is reframed in terms of credences and evidence, the
natural next question is how to act well under real-world uncertainty,
where full Bayesian calculation is computationally infeasible. Herbert
Simon's bounded rationality and satisficing describe agents who settle for
"good enough" given limited time and information; Kahneman and Tversky's
heuristics-and-biases program catalogs systematic ways human judgment
departs from ideal probabilistic reasoning; Gigerenzer's "ecological
rationality" pushes back, arguing many heuristics are well-adapted rather
than mere error.

**Status:** [ ]

**Resources:**
- Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*
- Gerd Gigerenzer, *Rationality for Mortals*
- Herbert Simon, *Models of Bounded Rationality*

## Naturalized Epistemology (`EP_NAT`)

Quine's "Epistemology Naturalized" proposed replacing the traditional
project of a priori justification of knowledge with descriptive cognitive
science: instead of asking how belief formation *ought* to work from first
principles, study how it actually does, as a chapter of psychology. This is
the natural end point of the tree's arc from "what is knowledge?" toward
practical world-modeling — the payoff isn't a proof that your beliefs are
justified in some ultimate sense, but a better empirical grip on which of
your actual belief-forming habits track reality and which don't.

**Status:** [ ]

**Resources:**
- W.V.O. Quine, "Epistemology Naturalized"
- Hilary Kornblith, *Naturalizing Epistemology*
- Alvin Goldman, *Epistemology and Cognition*
