## What Is Knowledge? Justified True Belief (`EP_INTRO`)

The classical (tripartite) analysis, traceable to Plato's *Theaetetus*,
holds that S knows that p just in case p is true, S believes that p, and S
is justified in believing that p. For most of the history of philosophy
this was treated as close to definitional rather than a live hypothesis —
the interesting work seemed to be in cashing out "justified." That
complacency ended abruptly in 1963 (see `EP_GETTIER`), but JTB is still the
right place to start: almost every later debate in the field is a debate
about which piece of this analysis is wrong, or missing.

## Philosophy of Language (`EP_LANG`)

Sitting between metaphysics and epistemology, philosophy of language matters
here because propositions — the things known, believed, and justified — are
usually individuated by their meaning. Frege's sense/reference distinction
explains how two co-referring terms can differ in cognitive content;
Wittgenstein's later "meaning as use" undercuts the idea that words simply
label pre-existing mental contents; and Quine's attack on the
analytic/synthetic distinction threatens to dissolve the very idea of a
clean line between what's true by meaning and what's true by fact — which
matters a great deal for what counts as a priori knowledge in `EP_RATEMP`.

**Resources:**
- Gottlob Frege, "On Sense and Reference"
- Ludwig Wittgenstein, *Philosophical Investigations*

## Sources of Knowledge: Perception / Reason / Testimony / Memory (`EP_SOURCES`)

The traditional taxonomy of where justified belief comes from. Perception
is the paradigm empirical source but is theory-laden and subject to
illusion; reason is supposed to deliver a priori knowledge but the scope of
that category is contested (see `EP_RATEMP`); testimony is arguably the
source of the vast majority of any individual's beliefs yet was long
under-theorized relative to perception; and memory is odd in that it seems
to preserve justification from an earlier source rather than generate it
fresh. Each source has its own generation of skeptical puzzles, and none of
them is obviously more basic than the others.

**Resources:**
- Robert Audi, *Epistemology: A Contemporary Introduction to the Theory of Knowledge*
