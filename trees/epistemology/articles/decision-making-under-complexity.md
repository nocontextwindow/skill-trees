# Decision-Making Under Complexity

[← Epistemology](../index.md) · `EP_COMPLEX`

The Bayesian ideal assumes unlimited computation, a well-defined hypothesis
space, and uncertainty that can be quantified. Real decisions violate all
three at once. The distinction between risk and Knightian uncertainty
matters here: some situations admit probabilities and some genuinely
don't, and treating the second kind as the first produces confident
nonsense. Bounded-rationality work makes a stronger claim than "we fall
short of the ideal" — that simple heuristics are not degraded
approximations of optimal rules but different rules, which can outperform
optimization in the environments they're matched to.

## Open questions

- When is a simple heuristic better than an explicit model — and can I tell
  which situation I'm in before the outcome?
- How should I act under uncertainty I genuinely can't put a number on,
  without pretending I can?
- Optimize for the expected case, or be robust to the worst one? What
  should decide which default I run?
- Deliberation has a cost. How do I decide how much to spend on deciding?
- Which of my decisions would actually change with better information? If
  few, what am I really doing when I gather more?
