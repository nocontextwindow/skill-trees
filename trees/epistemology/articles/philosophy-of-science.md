# Philosophy of Science

[← Epistemology](../index.md) · `EP_SCI`

The problem of induction has no accepted solution. Falsificationism was an
attempt to route around it and doesn't survive the Duhem-Quine point:
theories are only ever tested in bundles with auxiliary assumptions, so a
failed prediction never tells you which component to blame. What's left is
a set of genuinely live disputes — how theory choice works when the data
underdetermine it, whether the unobservable entities of successful theories
should be believed in or merely used, and what to make of the long list of
empirically successful theories that later turned out to be false.

## Open questions

- Does induction have a justification, or only a vindication — a reason to
  use it that isn't a reason to believe its conclusions?
- If theories are tested only in bundles, what exactly does an experiment
  confirm or refute?
- Should a theory's predictive success make me believe its unobservables
  exist, or only that it saves the phenomena?
- Given the historical record of discarded successful theories, how much
  credence do current ones deserve?
- Is there a demarcation criterion separating science from other
  belief-forming enterprises that survives examination?
