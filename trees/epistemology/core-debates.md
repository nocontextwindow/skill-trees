## The Gettier Problem (`EP_GETTIER`)

Edmund Gettier's three-page 1963 paper "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?"
produced counterexamples — a man justifiably but falsely believing "Jones
owns a Ford," inferring a disjunction that happens to be true for an
unrelated reason — where all three JTB conditions hold but intuition says
there's no knowledge, just lucky true belief. Decades of proposed fixes
followed: no-false-lemmas, causal theories, reliabilism, sensitivity and
safety conditions, virtue epistemology. None commands consensus, and
Gettier-style cases can seemingly be regenerated against almost any
proposed repair, which is itself a striking result about how slippery the
concept of knowledge is.

## Rationalism vs Empiricism (`EP_RATEMP`)

The great early-modern fault line: can substantive knowledge of the world
be reached through reason alone (Descartes, Leibniz, Spinoza — innate ideas,
a priori deduction from self-evident first principles), or must all content
ultimately derive from sense experience (Locke's tabula rasa, Berkeley,
Hume)? Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* tried to dissolve the dichotomy with
the synthetic a priori — judgments that are both informative about the
world and knowable independent of experience, grounded in the mind's own
structuring of experience (space, time, causality). Whether anything
genuinely synthetic a priori survives naturalism and Quine's later attack on
the analytic/synthetic distinction is still contested.

**Resources:**
- René Descartes, *Meditations on First Philosophy*
- David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*
- Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason* (Guyer & Wood translation; Paul Guyer's *Kant* is a good secondary guide)

## Skepticism (`EP_SKEPT`)

Cartesian skepticism (the evil demon, later the brain-in-a-vat) asks whether
any belief about an external world can survive the possibility of
systematic deception. Closure-based skeptical arguments exploit the fact
that if you know p and know that p entails q, you know q — but you don't
seem to know you're not a brain in a vat, so by closure you don't know you
have hands. Responses range from Moore's common-sense dismissal, to Nozick's
tracking/sensitivity conditions that deny closure, to contextualism (DeRose,
Lewis), which holds that "knows" shifts its truth conditions with
conversational stakes so that ordinary and skeptical claims aren't actually
in conflict.

**Resources:**
- René Descartes, *Meditations on First Philosophy* (Meditation I)
- Robert Nozick, *Philosophical Explanations* (the tracking theory)
- Keith DeRose, "Solving the Skeptical Problem"
