# Epistemology — Theories of Justification

## Theories of Justification (`EP_JUST`)

The organizing problem is the regress argument, sometimes called Agrippa's
trilemma: any belief offered as justification for another belief either
terminates in some foundational belief that needs no further justification,
loops back circularly on beliefs already in play, or regresses infinitely.
None of the three options is comfortable — infinite regress seems to give
no belief actual support, circularity looks viciously question-begging, and
foundationalism has to explain how a "basic" belief can be justified
without itself being justified by anything else. The three main theories
below (plus reliabilism, which sidesteps the regress by going externalist)
are the standard menu of answers.

## Foundationalism (`EP_FOUND`)

Justification terminates in "basic" beliefs that are non-inferentially
justified — self-evident truths, beliefs about one's own sense-data or
current mental states, or (in classical/Cartesian versions) beliefs
certain enough to be indubitable. Modest or moderate foundationalism
weakens this to beliefs that are merely non-inferentially warranted rather
than infallible, which is more defensible but raises the question of what
makes an unsupported belief warranted at all rather than an arbitrary
stopping point.

**Resources:**

## Coherentism (`EP_COH`)

Justification is holistic rather than linear: a belief is justified by its
fit within a web of mutually supporting beliefs, with no privileged
foundational tier. This dissolves the regress problem by denying that
justification is a linear chain at all, but faces the isolation objection —
a perfectly coherent belief system could in principle be entirely
disconnected from reality (a coherent fantasy) — and the related worry that
mere mutual support among beliefs doesn't obviously track truth.

**Resources:**

## Reliabilism (`EP_REL`)

Alvin Goldman's externalist departure from the internalist tradition: a
belief is justified (or counts as knowledge) if it's produced by a
reliable belief-forming process — one that produces a high ratio of true to
false beliefs — regardless of whether the believer has any internal access
to that reliability. This handles Gettier cases and animal/child cognition
gracefully but runs into the generality problem: any process token falls
under indefinitely many process types at different grains of description,
some reliable and some not, and there's no principled way to pick the
relevant type.

**Resources:**
- Alvin Goldman, "What Is Justified Belief?"
- Alvin Goldman, *Epistemology and Cognition*
