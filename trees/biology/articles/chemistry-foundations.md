# Chemistry Foundations

[← Biology](../index.md) · `BIO_CHEM`

The chemistry that biology is actually made of: water and its weirdness,
weak non-covalent interactions, acid-base equilibria, and the
thermodynamics and kinetics of reactions in crowded aqueous solution. The
non-obvious point is that almost nothing important in a cell is held
together by covalent bonds — structure, recognition, and regulation all run
on hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic effects, and van der Waals contacts, each
individually weaker than thermal noise and only meaningful in aggregate.
This is also why the standard chemistry-class picture misleads: reactions
in a cell happen at low concentrations, far from equilibrium, in a
molecularly crowded environment where diffusion and compartmentalization
matter as much as intrinsic reactivity, and where enzymes change rates by
factors of 10^10 or more without touching the equilibrium at all.

## Open questions

- How much chemistry do I actually need before biology stops being
  memorization, and where is the point of diminishing returns?
- Why does life run on such weak interactions, and what would a biology
  built on stronger bonds be unable to do?
- What does "the cell is far from equilibrium" actually buy it, and how
  much of biological order is bought with continuous energy expenditure?
- Where does the ball-and-stick picture of a molecule become actively
  misleading for thinking about biological function?
- Is the hydrophobic effect best thought of as attraction between nonpolar
  things or as water reorganizing itself — and does the distinction change
  any prediction?
- How much of biological specificity is chemistry and how much is just
  shape complementarity that any sufficiently large polymer could achieve?
