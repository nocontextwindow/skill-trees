# Polymers & Composites

[← Materials Science](../index.md) · `MAT_POLYMER`

Long-chain molecules and the engineered combinations built from them.
Polymers break most of the intuitions built on metals: properties depend on
chain length distribution and entanglement more than chemistry, behaviour
is viscoelastic so stiffness is a function of time and temperature rather
than a constant, and the glass transition is a kinetic phenomenon whose
measured value depends on how fast you measure it. Composites add
anisotropy and the fact that the interface between fibre and matrix
usually governs performance and failure. Composite failure is
progressive and multi-mode rather than a single yield event, which makes
inspection, damage tolerance, and end-of-life all genuinely harder than for
metals — and polymer degradation and recyclability are now first-order
design constraints rather than afterthoughts.

## Open questions

- What does "stiffness" even mean for a material whose modulus depends on
  loading rate and temperature — and how do I design against that?
- Is the glass transition a real phase transition or a kinetic arrest, and
  does the distinction change how I should use Tg values?
- How do you inspect and certify a structure whose failure is progressive
  and largely internal?
- When does the anisotropy of a composite become a liability rather than
  the point?
- How much should end-of-life recyclability weigh against in-service
  performance, and who is actually making that tradeoff?
- Where do metal-derived intuitions about fatigue and safety factors
  mislead badly for polymers?
