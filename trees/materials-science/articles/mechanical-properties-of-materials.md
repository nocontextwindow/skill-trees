# Mechanical Properties of Materials

[← Materials Science](../index.md) · `MAT_MECH`

Elasticity, plasticity, fracture, fatigue, and creep — how materials
deform and how they fail. Stiffness is a genuine material constant;
strength is not, and toughness even less so, because both depend on flaw
population, geometry, rate, and temperature. Fracture mechanics reframed
the whole question from "how strong is it" to "how big is the largest crack
and how fast does it grow," which is a much better question. Fatigue causes
most in-service failures and is inherently statistical, with scatter of
orders of magnitude even in nominally identical specimens; creep failure
dominates anything hot held under load for years. The strength-toughness
tradeoff is the standing tension: the same mechanisms that stop dislocations
also stop crack-tip plasticity.

## Open questions

- What does a single reported strength number actually mean, given the
  scatter and the geometry dependence behind it?
- Why is the strength-toughness tradeoff so persistent, and which materials
  genuinely beat it rather than trading elsewhere?
- How much do accelerated tests tell me about a twenty-year service life,
  and where does the extrapolation break?
- Is a safety factor an honest quantification of uncertainty or an
  institutional habit that hides how little is known?
- When should a design be built to tolerate a crack rather than to avoid
  one, and who decides?
- Where does a continuum description of deformation stop working, and what
  do I use instead at that scale?
