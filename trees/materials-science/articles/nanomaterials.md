# Nanomaterials

[← Materials Science](../index.md) · `MAT_NANO`

Materials where a dimension is small enough that surface area, quantum
confinement, or the absence of room for a defect changes the physics. The
genuine phenomena are real: quantum dots tune emission by size alone,
nanoscale metals show strength approaching theoretical limits because
dislocations have nowhere to multiply, and surface-dominated catalysis
behaves qualitatively differently from bulk. The genuine difficulty is
that almost nothing scales: the properties that make a single nanotube
remarkable rarely survive incorporation into a bulk composite, where
dispersion, alignment, and interface quality dominate. The field also has a
credibility problem from a decade of extrapolating single-object
measurements into material claims, and the toxicology and regulatory
picture remains genuinely unsettled.

## Open questions

- Why do nanomaterial properties so consistently fail to survive scale-up,
  and is that a manufacturing problem or a physical one?
- What is the honest track record here — which promised applications
  actually shipped, and what distinguished them?
- Where does "nano" mark a real change in physics versus just a small
  number in a marketing claim?
- How should I discount a result measured on a single object when the
  application requires a bulk quantity?
- Is nanoparticle toxicity assessable with existing frameworks, or does
  surface-area-dominated behaviour require different ones?
- Which properties actually improve monotonically as size decreases, and
  which have an optimum in the middle?
