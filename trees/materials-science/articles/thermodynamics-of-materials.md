# Thermodynamics of Materials

[← Materials Science](../index.md) · `MAT_THERMO`

Phase stability, phase diagrams, and the driving forces behind
transformations — plus the constant reminder that thermodynamics tells you
where a system wants to go and says nothing about whether it will get
there. Most engineering materials are metastable: steel, glass, and most
polymers are all thermodynamically wrong and kinetically stuck, and their
useful properties depend on staying that way. Phase diagrams are
equilibrium maps read by people working far from equilibrium, which is why
TTT and CCT diagrams exist. Diffusion sets the timescale for nearly
everything, and the gap between an equilibrium calculation and a real
processed part is filled entirely by kinetics.

## Open questions

- When is the equilibrium answer useful and when is it actively misleading
  for a real processed component?
- How do I reason about a metastable material's lifetime — what sets how
  long "stuck" lasts?
- Where does classical nucleation theory hold up, given how badly it fits
  measured nucleation rates?
- How much do computational thermodynamics databases actually extrapolate
  beyond the systems they were fitted to?
- Is there a clean way to see which transformations are diffusion-limited
  versus interface-limited without measuring?
- Where does thermodynamic reasoning stop being predictive for real
  engineered systems, and what replaces it there?
