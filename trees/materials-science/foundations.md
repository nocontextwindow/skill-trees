## Chemistry Foundations (`MAT_CHEM`)

The same general and physical chemistry that underlies biology also
underlies materials science, but the emphasis shifts toward bonding types
(metallic, ionic, covalent, van der Waals) and what each implies about bulk
behavior — metallic bonding gives you ductility and conductivity, ionic
bonding gives you hardness and brittleness, and so on. Periodic trends
(electronegativity, atomic radius, ionization energy) explain why certain
elements form the alloys, ceramics, and semiconductors they do. This is the
layer where "why is diamond hard but graphite soft" gets a real answer
(same element, different bonding geometry) rather than a memorized fact.

## Atomic & Crystal Structure (`MAT_STRUCT`)

Most engineering materials are crystalline, and the specific packing
arrangement — FCC, BCC, HCP, or something more exotic — sets hard limits on
density, slip systems, and mechanical behavior before you even get to
composition. Real crystals are never perfect: point defects (vacancies,
interstitials), line defects (dislocations), and planar defects (grain
boundaries) are what actually control plastic deformation, and dislocation
theory is arguably the single most load-bearing idea in the whole field —
it's the reason metals can be ductile at all instead of shattering like a
perfect crystal would. X-ray diffraction is the standard tool for
determining structure experimentally.
