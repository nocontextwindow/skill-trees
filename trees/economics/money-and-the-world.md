## Money & Monetary Policy (`ECON_MONEY`)

What money actually is (a medium of exchange, unit of account, and store of
value — and why that combination is harder to satisfy than it sounds), how
central banks create and manage it, and the tools (interest rates, open
market operations, quantitative easing) used to influence inflation and
employment. The Phillips curve — the historically observed tradeoff between
inflation and unemployment — is the central, contested organizing idea:
it held up reasonably well until the 1970s stagflation broke it, was
patched with expectations-augmented versions, and is still argued over
today (does raising rates reliably tame inflation without triggering a
recession, or is the relationship far looser than policymakers act like it
is?). This node is also the gateway to the "hard money" critique
(Austrian-school and crypto-adjacent arguments that central bank discretion
over the money supply is itself a source of instability) versus the
mainstream case for active monetary management.

**Resources:**
- Ben Bernanke, *21st Century Monetary Policy*
- Milton Friedman & Anna Schwartz, *A Monetary History of the United States*
- Friedrich Hayek, *Denationalisation of Money* (the strongest hard-money counter-case)

## International Trade (`ECON_TRADE`)

Why countries trade at all (comparative advantage — a country should
specialize in what it produces at the lowest *opportunity* cost, not
necessarily what it's best at in absolute terms) and what happens when
they do: gains from trade, exchange rate mechanics, trade balances, and the
real distributional effects that pure efficiency arguments tend to
undersell. The live tension worth understanding on its strongest terms:
economists broadly agree free trade raises aggregate welfare, but the
"China shock" literature (Autor, Dorn, Hanson) documented real, geographically
concentrated, persistent job losses from import competition that the
textbook model's frictionless reallocation assumption doesn't capture. That
gap between aggregate gains and concentrated losses is most of what
protectionist and industrial-policy arguments are actually about.

**Status:** [ ]

**Resources:**
- Paul Krugman, Maurice Obstfeld & Marc Melitz, *International Economics: Theory and Policy*
- David Autor, David Dorn & Gordon Hanson, "The China Shock" (working paper / *Annual Review of Economics*)
- Douglas Irwin, *Free Trade Under Fire*

## Development Economics (`ECON_DEV`)

Why some countries are rich and others are poor, and what — if anything —
policy can do to close the gap. Institutional economics (Acemoglu &
Robinson's *Why Nations Fail*) argues the deep driver is "extractive" vs.
"inclusive" political and economic institutions, largely set by colonial-era
path dependence; other camps emphasize geography (Jeffrey Sachs), culture,
or human capital directly. The field also had a genuine methodological
revolution over the last two decades: the randomista movement (Banerjee,
Duflo, Kremer — Nobel 2019) pushed development economics toward randomized
controlled trials of specific interventions (deworming, cash transfers,
microfinance) rather than grand unified theories of growth, trading
external validity for causal rigor. Both levels of the debate — grand
theory vs. randomized micro-interventions — are still very much live.

**Status:** [ ]

**Resources:**
- Daron Acemoglu & James Robinson, *Why Nations Fail*
- Abhijit Banerjee & Esther Duflo, *Poor Economics*
- William Easterly, *The Elusive Quest for Growth* (skeptical counterweight to both grand theories and aid orthodoxy)

## Political Economy (`ECON_POLECON`)

The seam where economics stops assuming policy is chosen by a benevolent,
capable planner and starts modeling the actual political incentives that
shape which policies get adopted: public choice theory (politicians and
bureaucrats are self-interested actors too), rent-seeking, regulatory
capture, and the median-voter model of why democracies converge toward
centrist policy. This is the node that explains why economically
"efficient" policies (congestion pricing, occupational licensing reform,
free trade) routinely lose to worse policies with better-organized,
more-concentrated beneficiaries — Mancur Olson's logic of concentrated
benefits versus diffuse costs. It's also the natural bridge into political
philosophy's questions about factions and coalition stability, since public
choice theory is essentially Federalist No. 10 formalized with
game-theoretic tools.

**Status:** [ ]

**Resources:**
- Mancur Olson, *The Logic of Collective Action*
- James Buchanan & Gordon Tullock, *The Calculus of Consent*
- Daron Acemoglu & James Robinson, *Economic Origins of Dictatorship and Democracy*
