## Game Theory (`ECON_GAME`)

The mathematics of strategic interaction — how rational agents should
behave when the outcome of their choice depends on what everyone else
chooses too. The core concept is Nash equilibrium (a set of strategies
where no player benefits from unilaterally deviating), and the canonical
teaching example, the Prisoner's Dilemma, demonstrates the field's most
important and unsettling result: individually rational choices can produce
a collectively worse outcome than cooperation would. Beyond the toy
examples, game theory underlies auction design (Nobel-winning work behind
spectrum auctions and ad markets), evolutionary biology (why cooperation
and altruism can be evolutionarily stable), and a good chunk of
international relations and negotiation theory. Repeated games — where the
same players interact many times — are where cooperation becomes rational
even among purely self-interested agents, via strategies like tit-for-tat.

## Behavioral Economics (`ECON_BEHAV`)

The empirical correction to classical economics' assumption of the
perfectly rational "homo economicus": real people exhibit predictable,
systematic deviations from rational-choice theory — loss aversion (losses
hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains feel good), present bias
(overweighting immediate rewards relative to future ones), anchoring,
framing effects, and mental accounting. Kahneman and Tversky's prospect
theory formalized these deviations well enough to reshape how policy
("nudges" — automatic enrollment in retirement plans is the flagship
example), marketing, and product design all operate. The live debate worth
tracking is how much of this is genuinely irrational versus how much is
actually well-adapted heuristic behavior for the environments humans
evolved in, applied to modern contexts (finance, advertising) those
heuristics weren't built for.
