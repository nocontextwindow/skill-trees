# Behavioral Economics

[← Economics](../index.md) · `ECON_BEHAV`

The documentation of systematic, predictable departures from
rational-choice models — loss aversion, framing effects, hyperbolic
discounting, and a long catalogue of others. The field's strength is
descriptive accuracy; its weaknesses are that many individual findings
replicated poorly, that a list of anomalies is not a theory, and that
whether a "bias" is an error depends on an assumed environment. It also
raises an awkward question for the rest of economics: if preferences can
be reversed by how a choice is presented, it is not obvious what welfare
analysis is measuring.

## Open questions

- Which findings survived replication, and what should a long list of
  individually small effects be taken to prove?
- Is a bias an error, or an adaptation to an environment different from
  the one in the lab?
- Do individual-level anomalies aggregate into market-level anomalies, or
  does competition wash them out?
- If preferences are constructed by framing, what is a welfare judgment
  even about?
- Where is the line between designing choice architecture and
  manipulating a choice — and who chooses the default?
- Does behavioral economics replace the rational-choice model or patch
  it? Is there a version that predicts rather than explains after the
  fact?
