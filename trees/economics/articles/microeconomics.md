# Microeconomics

[← Economics](../index.md) · `ECON_MICRO`

The theory of choice under scarcity, and of what happens when many such
choices meet in a market. The machinery — marginal reasoning, supply and
demand, equilibrium — is less interesting than the assumptions holding it
up: that preferences are stable and coherent, that agents optimize, that
markets clear. Each of those is known to be false in specific ways, and
the live question is always which failure matters for the problem in
front of you. The deepest claim in the subject is that prices carry
information no participant possesses individually, which is either the
most important result in economics or an overreach, depending on who you
ask.

## Open questions

- What work is the equilibrium assumption actually doing, and what
  conclusions survive if markets are never at rest?
- Are preferences a real feature of agents, or a description fitted to
  their choices after the fact? Does revealed preference explain anything
  or only redescribe it?
- Where does marginal analysis break down — lumpy goods, increasing
  returns, network effects — and how common are those cases really?
- How much of welfare economics survives the fact that utility isn't
  interpersonally comparable?
- Which market failures are structural, and which are transaction costs
  that a different institution could absorb?
- Is the price system a computation nothing else can replicate, or a
  heuristic that other information systems could now match?
