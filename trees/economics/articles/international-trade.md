# International Trade

[← Economics](../index.md) · `ECON_TRADE`

Why countries trade, what they gain, and who inside them loses. The
comparative advantage result is genuine and widely misunderstood: it
shows aggregate gains, says nothing about their distribution, and quietly
assumes factors can move to their next-best use. The last few decades
made the distributional footnote the main political fact, and revived
industrial policy arguments that trade theory had treated as settled.
Trade balances are a second source of confusion, since an accounting
identity is routinely presented as a diagnosis.

## Open questions

- The gains from trade are real but unevenly distributed — what makes
  compensating losers a theoretical footnote and a political
  impossibility?
- When is infant-industry or strategic protection right, and how would
  you know beforehand rather than in hindsight?
- Is a persistent trade deficit a symptom, a problem, or an identity
  mistaken for one?
- What does trade theory assume about factor mobility that no longer
  holds, and how much rests on that assumption?
- How should strategic dependency — chokepoints, single-source supply —
  be priced against efficiency?
- Does trade cause the political backlash attributed to it, or is it a
  legible target for displacement from technology and policy choices?
