## Microeconomics (`ECON_MICRO`)

How individual actors — consumers, firms, workers — make choices under
scarcity, and how those choices aggregate into market outcomes: supply and
demand, elasticity, consumer and producer surplus, marginal analysis, and
the conditions under which competitive markets are efficient (and where
they aren't — externalities, public goods, information asymmetry, and
market power). The core theoretical result worth understanding deeply is
the First Welfare Theorem (competitive equilibrium is Pareto efficient) and,
just as importantly, exactly which of its assumptions real markets violate,
since that's where the entire policy debate about regulation and market
failure actually lives. Marginal thinking — decisions are made at the
margin, not on averages or totals — is the single idea from this node that
transfers to the widest range of other fields.

## Macroeconomics (`ECON_MACRO`)

The economy viewed in aggregate: GDP and its components, unemployment,
inflation, business cycles, and the long-run drivers of economic growth.
This is also where the field's deepest and most consequential theoretical
disagreements live — Keynesians argue demand shortfalls and sticky prices
justify active fiscal and monetary intervention during downturns; classical
and real-business-cycle theorists argue markets clear efficiently on their
own and interventions mostly cause distortion; the debate over the 2008
financial crisis and the post-2020 inflation surge are both, at bottom,
re-runs of this argument with new data. Growth theory (why some countries
compound wealth for centuries and others stagnate — Solow's model and its
successors) is arguably the single most consequential question in all of
economics, since compounding growth rates over a century swamp almost any
other policy lever.
